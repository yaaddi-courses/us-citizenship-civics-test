# The Constitution and the idea of self-government

**Covers:** Republic, Representative democracy, Constitution, Consent of the governed, Amendment, Bill of Rights

## A government with written rules {#overview}
Every country has some way of deciding who makes the rules. The United States chose a particular answer: the people hold power, they choose representatives to use it, and a written document says what those representatives may and may not do. In this article we will follow that chain of ideas, from the broad idea to the document, and then see how the document can change.

## Republic {#republic}
Start with the broad idea. A republic is a country in which power comes from the people and leaders they elect use it, under a set of rules. It is the opposite of a monarchy, where the right to rule passes through a family. The United States has been a republic since its founding.

In a republic, no leader holds power for life only because of the family they were born into. Presidents, senators and representatives are elected, and they serve for limited terms.

## Representative democracy {#representative-democracy}
How do the people use their power? In a representative democracy, citizens vote for people who make laws on their behalf, and they do not vote on every single law. That is why it is also called indirect democracy. A direct democracy would ask all the voters to decide each issue, which is hard in a country of more than 330 million people.

The United States is both a republic and a representative democracy. The two words describe the same system from two angles. Republic points to elected leaders and written rules. Representative democracy points to the way the voters choose lawmakers.

## Constitution {#constitution}
The written rules themselves are the Constitution. It sets up the government, limits its powers and protects people's rights. It was written in 1787 and has been in effect since 1789. It is the highest law of the land, which means that no law may contradict it.

It has seven main articles. The first three describe the three branches of government. The others deal with the states, with changing the Constitution, with the supremacy of federal law and with how it was approved. It is short compared with many national constitutions, which is part of why courts and citizens still debate its meaning.

## Consent of the governed {#consent-of-the-governed}
Why should anyone obey a government? The founders answered with the consent of the governed: a government has the right to rule only because the people agree to be ruled. They stated it in 1776, when they broke away from British rule. Voting and peaceful elections are the main ways people give or withdraw that consent.

This also explains why free elections matter. If the voters cannot remove leaders, the government no longer rests on their agreement.

## Amendment {#amendment}
The founders knew the document would need changes, so they included a method. A change or addition to the text is an amendment. Two-thirds of both houses of the national legislature propose it, and then three-quarters of the states, which means 38 of 50, must approve it.

The bar is high on purpose. Only 27 amendments have been adopted since 1789, so the Constitution changes slowly and only with broad agreement.

## Bill of Rights {#bill-of-rights}
The first ten amendments, added in 1791, are called the Bill of Rights. They protect basic freedoms against the government. The First Amendment protects freedom of religion, speech, the press, assembly and petition. Other amendments protect people from unreasonable searches, guarantee a fair trial, and keep powers not given to the federal government for the states and the people.

Some states would approve the Constitution only if rights were added, which is why these amendments came right after it.

## A worked example {#example}
How many states must approve an amendment? Let's work it out with the figures above.

1. Total states: 50.
2. Required share: three-quarters, which is 0.75.
3. 50 times 0.75 equals 37.5.
4. States cannot be split, so we round up to 38.

Now turn the question around: how many states can block it? If 13 states say no, only 37 remain, and 37 is less than 38, so the amendment fails. A little more than a quarter of the states can stop any change.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is using "democracy" and "republic" as opposites. The United States is both.

A second slip is believing the Bill of Rights is separate from the Constitution. It is part of it, as the first ten amendments.

## Key points {#key-points}
- The United States is a republic and a representative democracy: leaders are elected under written rules.
- The Constitution is the supreme law, written in 1787, and can be changed only by amendment.
- An amendment needs two-thirds of both houses of the legislature and 38 of 50 states.
- The Bill of Rights, the first ten amendments, protects basic freedoms.
- The consent of the governed means the government rules because the people agree.
