# The president, the cabinet and the Supreme Court

**Covers:** Veto, Vetoes, Commander in Chief, Cabinet, Electoral College, Supreme Court

## Leading and judging {#overview}
Two other parts of the national government complete the picture alongside Congress. The president leads the executive branch and holds several roles at once. The highest court settles the hardest legal questions. Each has powers that are strong but limited, and each comes to office in a different way.

## Veto {#veto}
A veto is the president's power to reject a bill that Congress has passed, so that it does not become law. The president sends the bill back with the reasons for the refusal. Congress can still override a veto if two-thirds of the members in both houses vote for the bill again. Because that is difficult, a veto is often enough to stop a bill, and even the threat of one can change what Congress writes.

## Commander in Chief {#commander-in-chief}
Commander in Chief is the title of the president as the head of the nation's military. This ensures that an elected civilian, not a general, has the final say over the armed forces. Only Congress can declare war and provide the money for the military, so the two share the responsibility.

## Cabinet {#cabinet}
The cabinet is the group of department heads and top advisers who help the president. There are 15 executive departments, including State, Defense, Treasury and Justice, and each is led by a secretary. The president chooses the cabinet members, and the Senate must approve them. The vice president is also a member.

## Electoral College {#electoral-college}
The Electoral College is the system in which electors, chosen by each state's voters, formally elect the president. Each state gets as many electors as it has members of Congress: its House seats plus its two senators. The District of Columbia gets three. In total there are 538 electors, and a candidate needs a majority, which is 270, to win. In nearly every state, the candidate who wins the state's popular vote receives all of its electors.

The president serves a four-year term and may be elected no more than twice.

## Supreme Court {#supreme-court}
The Supreme Court is the highest court in the United States. It has nine justices, led by the Chief Justice. The president nominates them and the Senate approves them. They serve for life, which means until they retire, resign or are removed for wrongdoing. This frees them from pressure to please voters.

The court's most important power is to decide whether a law or government action agrees with the Constitution, called judicial review. Its decisions bind all lower courts, and they change only if the court itself reverses them or if an amendment changes the Constitution.

## A worked example {#example}
Where does the number 538 come from?

1. Each of the 435 House seats adds one elector, so the states start with 435.
2. The Senate has 100 members, which adds 100.
3. The District of Columbia gets 3.
4. Total: 435 + 100 + 3 = 538.
5. A majority of 538 is more than half, which is 270.

Check a state. California has 52 House seats and 2 senators, so it has 54 electors. Wyoming has 1 House seat and 2 senators, so it has 3. California has about 68 times the people of Wyoming but only 18 times the electors (54 divided by 3), so smaller states count for a bit more than their size alone would give them.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Believing the president is chosen directly by the national popular vote. The Electoral College makes it possible, though uncommon, for a candidate to win more votes nationwide and still lose. Another mistake is thinking justices serve a fixed term: they serve for life.

## Key points {#key-points}
- The president can veto a bill, and Congress can override with two-thirds of both houses.
- The president is the Commander in Chief and leads a cabinet of department heads.
- 538 electors choose the president, and 270 are needed to win.
- The Supreme Court has nine justices who serve for life and decide whether laws follow the Constitution.
