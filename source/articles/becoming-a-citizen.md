# Becoming a citizen: naturalization, promises and duties

**Covers:** Naturalization, Oath of Allegiance, Pledge of Allegiance, Civic participation, Selective Service

## From newcomer to citizen {#overview}
A person can be a US citizen by birth, or can become one later. This course is mainly for people who take the second path. The path has steps, and at the end comes a promise. After that, citizenship brings rights and also responsibilities. This article explains the process, the two well-known promises people make, and two duties that come with life in the United States.

## Naturalization {#naturalization}
Naturalization is the legal process by which a person born in another country becomes a US citizen. It is run by US Citizenship and Immigration Services.

In outline, the applicant must be an adult who has lived in the country as a lawful permanent resident for a set time, usually five years, or three years if married to a US citizen. They must show good moral character, speak and read basic English, and pass a test on US history and government. That test is the one this course prepares you for. There is also an interview, and the final step is a ceremony.

## Oath of Allegiance {#oath-of-allegiance}
The Oath of Allegiance is the promise new citizens make at the naturalization ceremony. In it they promise to support and defend the Constitution and the laws of the United States, to be loyal to the country, and to give up loyalty to any other country. They also promise to serve the country when the law requires it, for example by performing military or civilian service in some cases.

Only after the oath does a person become a citizen. Until then, they are still an applicant, even after they have passed the test.

## Pledge of Allegiance {#pledge-of-allegiance}
The Pledge of Allegiance is a short promise of loyalty to the flag and to the nation. Many Americans say it in schools, at government meetings and at public events, usually standing with a hand over the heart.

It is easy to mix up with the oath. The pledge is a custom: anyone can say it, as often as they like, and it is not a legal requirement. The oath is a formal promise that changes someone's legal status once. The pledge was first written in 1892, and its wording has been changed a few times since.

## Civic participation {#civic-participation}
Civic participation means taking part in public life. Voting is the best-known way, but it is not the only one. Others include serving on a jury, paying taxes, joining a neighbourhood group, attending a local meeting, writing to an elected official and volunteering.

A healthy republic depends on this involvement. If few people take part, a small group makes the decisions for everyone. Non-citizens can also take part in many of these ways, such as volunteering, even before they can vote.

## Selective Service {#selective-service}
Selective Service is the system that registers men between the ages of 18 and 25 in case Congress ever brings back a military draft. There has been no draft since 1973, but the registration is still the law, and most men must sign up within 30 days of turning 18. Both citizens and many immigrants must register. Failing to do so can affect things like federal student aid and, for immigrants, later naturalization.

## A worked example {#example}
A woman moves to the United States and becomes a lawful permanent resident on 1 March 2020. Here is a sample timeline:

1. 1 March 2020: she becomes a permanent resident.
2. 1 December 2024: she can apply for naturalization, because the rule allows applying up to 90 days before the five-year mark.
3. About six to twelve months later: she attends an interview and takes the English and civics tests.
4. A few weeks after approval: she goes to the ceremony and takes the Oath of Allegiance. She is now a citizen.
5. The same year: she can register to vote, and she may be called for jury duty.

The dates are illustrations. Processing times vary, so check the official website for the current rules.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Confusing the pledge and the oath. The pledge is a custom that anyone may say. The oath is a legal step that only new citizens take. Another mistake is assuming that Selective Service is a draft: it is only a list of names, and nobody is called up unless Congress and the President decide to restart a draft.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Naturalization is the process of becoming a citizen after birth in another country, and it ends with the oath.
- The Oath of Allegiance is the formal promise that makes someone a citizen; the Pledge of Allegiance is a custom.
- Civic participation includes voting but also jury service, volunteering and contacting officials.
- Selective Service is a registration for men aged 18 to 25, not an active draft.
