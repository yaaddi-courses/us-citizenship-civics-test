# Becoming a citizen: naturalization, promises and duties

**Covers:** Naturalization, Oath of Allegiance, Pledge of Allegiance, Civic participation, Selective Service

## From newcomer to citizen {#overview}
A person can be a US citizen by birth, or can become one later. If you are studying with this course, you are probably on the second path. That path has steps, and at the end comes a promise. After the promise, citizenship brings rights and also responsibilities. In this article we will walk through the process, the two well-known promises people make, and two ways of taking part in American life.

## Naturalization {#naturalization}
Suppose you were born in another country and want to become a US citizen. The legal process you follow is called naturalization. It is run by US Citizenship and Immigration Services.

In outline, you must be an adult who has lived in the country as a lawful permanent resident for a set time, usually five years, or three years if you are married to a US citizen. You must show good moral character, speak and read basic English, and pass a test on US history and government. That test is the one this course prepares you for. There is also an interview, and the final step is a ceremony.

## Oath of Allegiance {#oath-of-allegiance}
At the ceremony, new citizens make a promise called the Oath of Allegiance. In it they promise to support and defend the Constitution and the laws of the United States, to be loyal to the country, and to give up loyalty to any other country. They also promise to serve the country when the law requires it, for example by performing military or civilian service in some cases.

Notice the order. A person becomes a citizen only after the oath. Until then they are still an applicant, even after they have passed the test.

## Pledge of Allegiance {#pledge-of-allegiance}
You will also hear about a second promise, the Pledge of Allegiance. It is a short promise of loyalty to the flag and to the nation. Many Americans say it in schools, at government meetings and at public events, usually standing with a hand over the heart.

It is easy to mix up with the oath, so let's separate them. The pledge is a custom: anyone can say it, as often as they like, and it is not a legal requirement. The oath is a formal promise that changes someone's legal status once. The pledge was first written in 1892, and its wording has been changed a few times since.

## Civic participation {#civic-participation}
Citizenship is more than a certificate. Taking part in public life is called civic participation. Voting is the best-known way, but it is not the only one. Others include serving on a jury, paying taxes, joining a neighbourhood group, attending a local meeting, writing to an elected official and volunteering.

Why does it matter? A healthy republic depends on this involvement. If few people take part, a small group makes the decisions for everyone. You do not have to wait for your citizenship either. Non-citizens can take part in many of these ways, such as volunteering, even before they can vote.

## Selective Service {#selective-service}
One duty surprises many newcomers. Selective Service is the system that registers men between the ages of 18 and 25, in case Congress ever brings back a military draft. There has been no draft since 1973, but the registration is still the law, and most men must sign up within 30 days of turning 18. Both citizens and many immigrants must register. Failing to do so can affect things like federal student aid and, for immigrants, later naturalization.

## A worked example {#example}
Let's follow a woman who moves to the United States and becomes a lawful permanent resident on 1 March 2020.

1. 1 March 2020: she becomes a permanent resident.
2. 1 December 2024: she can apply for naturalization, because the rule allows applying up to 90 days before the five-year mark. Count it: five years ends on 1 March 2025, and December (31 days), January (31) and February (28) add up to 90 days.
3. About six to twelve months later: she attends an interview and takes the English and civics tests.
4. A few weeks after approval: she goes to the ceremony and takes the Oath of Allegiance. She is now a citizen.
5. The same year: she can register to vote, and she may be called for jury duty.

The dates are illustrations. Processing times vary, so check the official website for the current rules.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is confusing the pledge and the oath. The pledge is a custom that anyone may say. The oath is a legal step that only new citizens take.

A second slip is assuming that Selective Service is a draft. It is only a list of names, and nobody is called up unless Congress and the President decide to restart a draft.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Naturalization is the process of becoming a citizen after birth in another country, and it ends with the oath.
- The Oath of Allegiance is the formal promise that makes someone a citizen; the Pledge of Allegiance is a custom.
- Civic participation includes voting but also jury service, volunteering and contacting officials.
- Selective Service is a registration for men aged 18 to 25, not an active draft.
