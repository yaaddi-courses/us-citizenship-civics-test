# Turning points in American history

**Covers:** Taxation without representation, Federalist Papers, Emancipation Proclamation, Reconstruction, Great Depression, Cold War, Civil rights movement

## A short tour in time {#overview}
The civics test asks about history as well as government, and the history questions tend to circle around a handful of moments when the country changed direction. This article covers seven of them in the order they happened, from the first protests against Britain to the struggle for equal rights in the 1960s. For each one, learn what happened, roughly when, and why it mattered.

## Taxation without representation {#taxation-without-representation}
Taxation without representation was the colonists' protest that Britain's taxes were unfair, because they had no voice in the British government. After the costly French and Indian War, Britain passed taxes such as the Stamp Act of 1765 and the Tea Act of 1773. Colonists protested with the slogan "No taxation without representation". In 1773 a group in Boston dumped a cargo of tea into the harbour, which became known as the Boston Tea Party. The conflict led to the American Revolution.

## Federalist Papers {#federalist-papers}
The Federalist Papers are 85 essays that Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and John Jay wrote in 1787 and 1788 to persuade New Yorkers to approve the new Constitution. They signed with the pen name "Publius". The essays explain why a strong national government with checks and balances would protect liberty. They are still read today to understand what the founders intended.

## Emancipation Proclamation {#emancipation-proclamation}
The Emancipation Proclamation was the order issued by President Abraham Lincoln on 1 January 1863 that freed enslaved people in the states fighting against the Union. It did not free everyone at once, because it applied only to areas in rebellion and the Union army had to win them. But it made ending slavery a goal of the Civil War and allowed Black men to join the Union army. Slavery was ended in the whole country in 1865 by the 13th Amendment.

## Reconstruction {#reconstruction}
Reconstruction was the period after the Civil War, from 1865 to 1877, when the country rebuilt the South and expanded the rights of formerly enslaved people. Three amendments were passed: the 13th ended slavery, the 14th made them citizens with equal protection, and the 15th said that the right to vote could not be denied because of race. Reconstruction ended when federal troops withdrew, and many Southern states then passed laws that took those rights away in practice.

## Great Depression {#great-depression}
The Great Depression was the longest and worst economic downturn of the 1900s. It began with the stock market crash of October 1929. Banks failed, businesses closed, and by 1933 about one in four workers had no job. President Franklin D. Roosevelt answered with the New Deal, a set of programs that created jobs and added protections such as Social Security.

## Cold War {#cold-war}
The Cold War was the long tense rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union after World War II. It was called cold because the two never fought each other directly. Instead they competed in an arms race and a space race and backed opposite sides in conflicts such as Korea and Vietnam. The Berlin Wall fell in 1989, and the Soviet Union broke up in 1991.

## Civil rights movement {#civil-rights-movement}
The civil rights movement was the struggle, mostly in the 1950s and 1960s, to end racial discrimination and win equal rights for Black Americans. It used peaceful protests, boycotts and court cases. Key moments include the Supreme Court's 1954 decision that segregated schools are unequal, the Montgomery bus boycott in 1955, and the March on Washington in 1963, where Martin Luther King Jr. gave his "I Have a Dream" speech. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 turned its goals into law.

## A worked example {#example}
Put the seven events on a timeline and work out the gaps:

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1765 | Stamp Act: "no taxation without representation" |
| 1788 | Federalist Papers help win approval of the Constitution |
| 1863 | Emancipation Proclamation |
| 1865 to 1877 | Reconstruction |
| 1929 | Stock market crash starts the Great Depression |
| 1947 to 1991 | Cold War |
| 1954 to 1965 | Civil rights movement's major victories |

The gap from the Stamp Act to the Declaration in 1776 is 11 years. Reconstruction lasted 12 years. The Cold War lasted 44 years, longer than any other event on the list. The Depression, the Cold War and the civil rights movement all overlap in the twentieth century, so the history of those decades has to be read side by side, not one after another.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Believing that the Emancipation Proclamation ended slavery everywhere on 1 January 1863. It freed people only in rebel states, and the 13th Amendment finished the job in 1865. Another common slip is mixing the order of the Great Depression and the Cold War: the first comes before World War II, the second after it.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Taxation without representation was a main cause of the Revolution, in the 1760s and 1770s.
- The Federalist Papers argued for approving the Constitution.
- The Emancipation Proclamation (1863) and the 13th Amendment (1865) moved the country toward ending slavery.
- Reconstruction (1865 to 1877) tried to rebuild the South and protect new citizens' rights.
- The Great Depression, the Cold War and the civil rights movement shaped the twentieth century.
