# Three branches and their limits

**Covers:** Separation of powers, Checks and balances, Legislative branch, Executive branch, Judicial branch

## Why split the power {#overview}
The people who wrote the Constitution had lived under a king, and they feared giving one person or group too much power. Their solution was to divide the government into three parts, each with its own job, and to let each part limit the others. The idea is simple: if no part can act alone, no part can easily take over.

## Separation of powers {#separation-of-powers}
Separation of powers means dividing government into branches with different jobs, so that power is not held in one place. The first three articles of the Constitution describe the three branches in turn. The people in one branch cannot normally hold a job in another. A member of Congress, for example, cannot also serve as a judge.

## Legislative branch {#legislative-branch}
The legislative branch writes the laws. At the national level it is Congress, which has two parts: the Senate and the House of Representatives. Its members are elected by the voters. Congress also controls the federal budget, which means that it decides how tax money is spent, and it can declare war.

## Executive branch {#executive-branch}
The executive branch carries out the laws. Its leader is the president, who is supported by the vice president and by many departments and agencies. When Congress passes a law about, say, food safety, the executive branch makes the rules for businesses and checks that they follow them. The president also manages relations with other countries.

## Judicial branch {#judicial-branch}
The judicial branch explains the laws and settles disputes about them. It is made up of the courts, with the highest at the top. Judges decide what a law means, whether someone has broken it, and whether a law agrees with the Constitution. Federal judges are not elected. The president appoints them and the Senate approves them, which protects them from voter pressure.

## Checks and balances {#checks-and-balances}
Checks and balances is the system in which each branch can limit the powers of the others, so that none becomes too strong. Some examples:
- The president can refuse to sign a bill, which stops it from becoming law.
- Congress can override that refusal if two-thirds of both houses vote for the bill again.
- Courts can decide that a law or an order breaks the Constitution, and then it cannot be enforced.
- The Senate must approve the president's choices for judges and for top officials.
- Congress can remove a president or judge who commits serious wrongdoing through impeachment.

## A worked example {#example}
Follow a bill through the system, and count the votes needed to overcome a refusal:

1. The House of Representatives passes a bill.
2. The Senate passes the same bill.
3. The president refuses to sign it. This is the executive check on the legislative branch.
4. Congress tries to override. It needs two-thirds of each house.
5. In the Senate, two-thirds of 100 is 66.7, so 67 votes are needed when all senators vote. In the House, two-thirds of 435 is exactly 290.
6. If both votes succeed, the bill becomes law. Later, a court might still decide that it breaks the Constitution, which is the judicial check.

Each step involves a different branch, and the final word does not belong to any single one.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Assuming that the president can make any law. The president can propose and sign laws but cannot write them. Another mistake is thinking that courts make laws. Courts decide what laws mean and whether they follow the Constitution, but they do not pass new ones.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Separation of powers divides government into legislative, executive and judicial branches.
- The legislative branch (Congress) writes laws, the executive branch (the president) carries them out, and the judicial branch (the courts) explains them.
- Checks and balances let each branch limit the others.
- Overriding a presidential refusal takes two-thirds of both houses.
